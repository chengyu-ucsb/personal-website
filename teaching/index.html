Teaching

Start with a problem students can recognize.

I teach organizational communication, research methods, and theory by connecting scholarship to decisions people make at work and in everyday life.

Approach

A supervisor communicates differently than you do. An organization’s stated values clash with its practices. An AI tool gives an answer no one knows how to verify. I often begin with a problem like these, then ask students to use theory and evidence to explain what is happening.

I build assignments in stages so students can test an idea, get feedback, and revise it. As someone who learned to navigate academic expectations as a first-generation student, I want those expectations to be clear enough that students can challenge them and make the work their own.

In the classroom
Organizations in practice

Learn from people doing the work

In organizational communication, students interview working professionals and analyze their experiences with course concepts. In global communication, they follow a multinational organization and write a comparative white paper. At Cincinnati, client teams develop a public relations and corporate social responsibility packet for a local organization, with feedback before the final pitch.

Methods & AI

Ask what the evidence can support

In research methods, students move through small studies using surveys, experiments, qualitative approaches, and content analysis. With support from a Designing Learning with AI grant, I added exercises that ask them to check AI claims against original sources, document where a tool helps or misleads, and reflect on ethical use.

Graduate learning

Practice the work of a scholar

In advanced theory, graduate students rotate among three roles: synthesizing a body of readings, presenting an article in conference style, and writing a journal-style peer review. The roles give students different ways to reconstruct, question, and extend a theory together.
